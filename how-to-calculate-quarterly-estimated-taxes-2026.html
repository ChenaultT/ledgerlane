How to Calculate Quarterly Estimated Taxes for 2026 (Freelancers and 1099 Contractors)

When you work for yourself, nobody withholds tax from your pay. The IRS still expects tax to be paid as you earn, so most freelancers, 1099 contractors and sole proprietors make four estimated payments a year. This guide shows how to work out what to pay for 2026, step by step, using the figures in the IRS's 2026 Form 1040-ES.

Not tax advice. This is a simplified walkthrough for US federal tax. It doesn't cover every credit, deduction or limit. Check Form 1040-ES and Publication 505, or ask a qualified tax professional. Figures checked on irs.gov on October 14, 2026.

Do you need to pay estimated tax?

Per the IRS, individuals generally must make estimated payments if they expect to owe $1,000 or more when they file, after subtracting withholding and refundable credits. You don't have to pay estimated tax for 2026 if you had no tax liability for 2025, were a US citizen or resident for all of 2025, and your 2025 tax year covered 12 months.

If you also have a W-2 job, raising your withholding with a new Form W-4 can replace some or all estimated payments.

The 2026 due dates

Payment Covers income from Due
1 Jan 1 – Mar 31 April 15, 2026
2 Apr 1 – May 31 June 15, 2026
3 Jun 1 – Aug 31 September 15, 2026
4 Sep 1 – Dec 31 January 15, 2027

The "quarters" aren't equal: the second covers two months and the fourth covers four. You don't have to make the January 15, 2027 payment if you file your 2026 return by February 1, 2027 and pay the full balance with it. If a due date falls on a weekend or legal holiday, the payment is on time if made the next business day.

Step 1: Estimate your 2026 net profit

Start with business income for the year minus deductible business expenses. This is your expected Schedule C net profit. Mid-year, a simple approach is to annualize: if you've earned $45,000 of profit by the end of August, that's 8 of 12 months, so a rough full-year projection is $45,000 ÷ 8 × 12 = $67,500. Adjust for anything you know is coming, such as a slow December or a big contract.

Step 2: Calculate self-employment tax

Self-employment (SE) tax is Social Security and Medicare for the self-employed. The 2026 Form 1040-ES worksheet does it like this:

  1. Multiply net profit by 92.35%.
  2. Medicare part: that amount × 2.9%.
  3. Social Security part: that amount, up to the $184,500 2026 wage base (minus any W-2 wages), × 12.4%.
  4. SE tax = Medicare part + Social Security part (15.3% combined below the wage base).

Half of your SE tax is deductible when figuring adjusted gross income. If your SE earnings plus wages exceed $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly), the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax may also apply.

Step 3: Estimate your federal income tax

  1. AGI ≈ net profit − half of SE tax − other adjustments (such as SE health insurance or a SEP/solo 401(k) contribution) + any other income.
  2. Subtract your deduction. The 2026 standard deduction is $16,100 single or married filing separately, $32,200 married filing jointly, $24,150 head of household.
  3. Qualified business income (QBI) deduction. Many sole proprietors can deduct up to 20% of qualified business income, limited to 20% of taxable income before the deduction. Higher-income taxpayers face further limits.
  4. Apply the 2026 tax rate schedule to the result. For single filers, 2026 starts at 10% up to $12,400, then 12% up to $50,400, then 22% up to $105,700.
  5. Subtract any credits you expect.

Step 4: Add it up and apply the safe harbor

Total estimated tax = income tax + SE tax (+ any other taxes) − withholding.

You don't have to predict your tax perfectly. You can generally avoid the underpayment penalty by paying, through withholding and timely estimates, the smaller of:

  • 90% of your 2026 tax, or
  • 100% of the tax shown on your 2025 return (110% if your 2025 AGI was more than $150,000, or $75,000 if married filing separately). This option requires a 12-month 2025 return.

Using last year's tax is popular with freelancers whose income is rising, because the number is known in advance. If your income dropped, 90% of this year's estimate may be lower.

Divide the required amount by four and pay each installment by its due date. Paying late can trigger a penalty even if you're due a refund at filing time.

A worked example

Single filer, no other income, projected 2026 net profit of $60,000, standard deduction, simplified QBI deduction:

Step Amount
Net earnings from self-employment ($60,000 × 92.35%) $55,410
SE tax ($55,410 × 15.3%) $8,478
Deductible half of SE tax $4,239
AGI ($60,000 − $4,239) $55,761
Minus standard deduction −$16,100
Taxable income before QBI $39,661
QBI deduction (20% of $55,761 = $11,152, capped at 20% of $39,661) −$7,932
Taxable income $31,729
Income tax ($1,240 + 12% × ($31,729 − $12,400)) $3,559
Total estimated 2026 federal tax $12,037
Each quarterly payment (÷ 4) ≈ $3,009

That's about 20% of profit for this example. A higher income, a state income tax or other income would change it, which is why a fixed "set aside X%" rule of thumb can be off. If this person's 2025 total tax had been $10,000, the prior-year safe harbor would be $10,000 (100%, since AGI was under $150,000), or $2,500 a quarter, lower than 90% of this year's estimate ($10,833).

Step 5: Pay, record and re-check each quarter

  • Pay through IRS Direct Pay, your IRS online account, EFTPS, or by mail with a 1040-ES voucher. You can pay more often than quarterly, as long as enough is paid by each due date.
  • Record the date, amount and confirmation number of every payment. You'll need the total when you file.
  • Re-estimate each quarter. If income jumps, recalculate and raise the remaining payments. Uneven income may qualify for the annualized income installment method on Form 2210, Schedule AI.
  • Don't forget your state. Most states with an income tax have their own estimated payments, and dates and rules vary.

Habits that make this easier

  • Move a percentage of every client payment into a separate tax savings account the day it arrives.
  • Log income and expenses monthly, not once a year.
  • Keep 1099-NEC and 1099-K forms with your records, but report all income whether or not you receive a form.
