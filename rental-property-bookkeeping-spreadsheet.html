Rental Property Bookkeeping Spreadsheet: How to Set One Up the Schedule E Way

If you own one to ten rentals, you probably don't need accounting software. You need a spreadsheet you'll actually keep up with, organized so that at year end the totals drop straight into the categories your tax preparer asks for.

This guide shows how to structure a rental property bookkeeping spreadsheet, which categories to use, how to handle the tricky items (deposits, advance rent, repairs vs. improvements, mileage), and a monthly routine that takes about 20 minutes.

General information, not tax advice. Rules referenced are from IRS Publication 527 and IRS Topic 415. Your preparer decides how items are reported.

Start with the end in mind: Schedule E

Most individual landlords report residential rental income and expenses on Schedule E (Form 1040), Part I, one column per property. Its expense lines are a ready-made category list:

Line Category
5 Advertising
6 Auto and travel
7 Cleaning and maintenance
8 Commissions
9 Insurance
10 Legal and other professional fees
11 Management fees
12 Mortgage interest paid to banks, etc.
13 Other interest
14 Repairs
15 Supplies
16 Taxes
17 Utilities
18 Depreciation expense or depletion
19 Other

If every expense you log is tagged with one of these categories and a property, your year-end summary is just a filtered total.

The tabs your spreadsheet needs

1. Properties (setup). One row per property: a short nickname, address and property type. Every other tab uses the nickname in a dropdown, so you never type an address twice and totals can't split across spelling variations.

2. Rent roll. One row per unit: tenant, lease start and end, monthly rent, security deposit held. Add a formula that flags leases ending within the next 60 days so renewals don't sneak up on you.

3. Income log. One row per payment received: date, property, unit, amount, type (rent, late fee, pet rent, laundry, deposit kept) and method. Log money when it is received, not when it was due.

4. Rent tracker. A unit-by-month grid that sums the income log, so you can see who has paid, who paid part and who hasn't, with conditional colors. This is the tab you'll check most often.

5. Expense log. Date, property, vendor, description, amount, category and a receipt link or note. The Schedule E line fills in automatically from the category. Add an "all properties" option for shared costs like software or tax prep, split across properties.

6. Mileage log. Date, property, purpose and miles, with the IRS rate for that date.

7. Summary. A per-property table of rents received and lines 5–19, plus a P&L by month.

The tricky items, handled correctly

Security deposits. According to IRS Publication 527, don't include a security deposit in income when you receive it if you plan to return it at the end of the lease. If you keep part or all of it because the tenant didn't meet the lease terms, include the amount you keep in income for that year. In your spreadsheet, track held deposits on the rent roll and log only kept deposits as income.

Advance rent. Pub. 527 says to include advance rent in rental income in the year you receive it, regardless of the period it covers. If a tenant pays January's rent in December, it belongs to December's year.

Repairs vs. improvements. Repairs that keep the property in working condition (fixing a leak, patching drywall) are generally deductible. Pub. 527 says an expense is an improvement, which must be capitalized and depreciated, if it results in a betterment, a restoration or an adaptation to a new use. A new roof or a kitchen remodel is generally an improvement. Keep a separate "capital improvement" category that is tracked but kept out of the repairs line, and give your preparer the list.

Mortgage payments. Only the interest portion goes on line 12. Principal isn't a deductible expense, so log it in a non-deductible category if you want your cash flow to reconcile.

Mileage. For 2026, the IRS business standard mileage rate is 72.5 cents/mile for January 1–June 30 and 76 cents/mile for July 1–December 31 (IRS standard mileage rates). Because the rate changed mid-year, your mileage log should pick the rate by trip date rather than using a single number. Keep date, destination and purpose for every trip.

Personal use. If you or your family also use a property, IRS Topic 415 says you're treated as using it as a residence if personal-use days exceed the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days it's rented at a fair price, and expenses may then be limited. Track rental days and personal days per property.

A 20-minute monthly routine

  1. Log rent received from your bank or payment app into the income log.
  2. Check the rent tracker for partial or missing payments and follow up.
  3. Log expenses from your bank and card statements, assigning a category and property to each. Attach or file receipts.
  4. Add mileage from your calendar or notes.
  5. Glance at the rent roll for leases ending soon.
  6. Review the monthly P&L for anything unusual, such as a utility bill that doubled.

At year end, the summary tab gives you per-property totals by Schedule E line, ready to hand to your preparer along with depreciation records and Form 1098s.

Tips that save headaches

  • Use a separate bank account for your rentals if you can. Reconciliation becomes far easier.
  • One spreadsheet per tax year. Copy last year's file, clear the logs and keep your properties and rent roll.
  • Keep records. The IRS says to keep records that support your return until its period of limitations runs out (generally 3 years after filing), and records connected to the property itself (purchase, improvements, depreciation) until the period of limitations expires for the year you sell or dispose of it (IRS: How long should I keep records?).
  • Don't over-categorize. Fifteen categories that match Schedule E beat forty custom ones you'll have to regroup in April.
