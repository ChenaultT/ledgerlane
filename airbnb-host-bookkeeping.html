Airbnb Host Bookkeeping: What to Track, How to Track It, and the IRS Rules to Know

Short-term rental income looks simple from the host dashboard: a booking comes in, a payout lands a few days later. But the payout is already net of platform fees, it may include cleaning fees, some stays cross month-end, and your real costs (turnovers, supplies, utilities, insurance, mortgage interest) live somewhere else entirely. Without a system, it's hard to answer the basic questions: Is this listing actually profitable? Which months carry the year? Is my pricing working?

This guide lays out a bookkeeping system that answers those questions and leaves you with clean numbers for tax time. It works for Airbnb, Vrbo, Booking.com and direct bookings, for one listing or ten.

Track bookings at the gross level, not just payouts

The most common mistake is logging only the payout. A payout hides what guests paid, what the platform took and what was refunded. For every reservation, record:

  • Listing and platform (or "direct")
  • Confirmation code and guest name
  • Check-in and check-out dates (nights = check-out − check-in)
  • Accommodation amount (the nightly-rate total)
  • Cleaning fee and any other guest fees
  • Discounts or refunds
  • Platform or host service fee
  • Taxes you collected yourself, if any (more on this below)
  • Actual payout and payout date
  • Status: upcoming, completed or cancelled

Gross revenue = accommodation + cleaning + other fees − discounts. Expected payout = gross revenue − platform fee (+ any tax you collected yourself). The platform's reservation or earnings details show these amounts.

Reconcile payouts every month

Once you log the components, add a payout check column: expected payout minus actual payout. Anything other than zero deserves a look. It could be a typo, a resolution-center adjustment, a currency difference or a fee you didn't expect. Catching it the same month is far easier than in April.

Many platforms also offer an earnings or transaction-history export. Use it to make sure every payout in your bank account matches a booking in your log.

Handle occupancy and lodging taxes carefully

In many places, short-term stays are subject to occupancy, lodging or tourist taxes. Depending on your location, the platform may collect and remit them for you, or you may have to collect and remit them yourself, especially on direct bookings. Check your local rules.

  • If the platform collects and remits the tax, it isn't part of your revenue. Leave it out of your income figures.
  • If you collect it, record it separately from revenue. It's money you hold for the tax authority. Log the remittance as a pass-through item, not as an operating expense.

Log expenses by listing, with a category

Expenses for a short-term rental look a lot like a long-term rental's, plus turnover costs. Typical categories line up with the Schedule E expense lines: advertising, auto and travel, cleaning and maintenance, commissions, insurance, legal and professional fees, management (co-host) fees, mortgage interest, other interest, repairs, supplies, taxes, utilities, depreciation and other (for example, permits, HOA dues and pricing software).

Tag each expense to a listing. For costs shared across listings, such as a pricing tool or tax preparation, split them evenly or by another reasonable method, and do it the same way all year.

Keep a few things out of deductible expenses but still tracked: furniture and other items that need to be depreciated, mortgage principal, lodging tax you remit, and personal spending.

The performance metrics worth calculating

Three numbers from the hotel industry tell you more than revenue alone:

  • Occupancy rate = booked nights ÷ available nights. Available nights are the nights the listing was open, minus owner stays and blocked nights. Count only up to today, so a mid-year figure isn't diluted by future dates.
  • ADR (average daily rate) = accommodation revenue ÷ booked nights. Leave out cleaning and other fees so ADR reflects your nightly pricing.
  • RevPAR (revenue per available night) = accommodation revenue ÷ available nights. It combines price and occupancy. If you cut prices and occupancy rises, RevPAR shows whether you came out ahead.

Calculate them by listing and by month. Stays that cross a month boundary should be split by the calendar so each month gets its own nights.

The IRS rules every host should flag

These rules come from official IRS sources. How they apply to you depends on your facts, so treat them as flags to raise with your tax professional, not conclusions.

Personal use (the 14-day / 10% rule). Per IRS Topic 415, you're treated as using a dwelling as a residence if your personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days it's rented at a fair rental price. In that case, deductible rental expenses may be limited. Personal use includes days used by you, co-owners and family members, and days rented below a fair price. Log owner stays for this reason.

Rented fewer than 15 days. Also per Topic 415, if you use a dwelling as a residence and rent it for fewer than 15 days in the year, you don't report the rental income and don't deduct rental expenses.

Schedule E or Schedule C. Publication 527 says that if you provide substantial services primarily for your guests' convenience, such as regular cleaning, changing linen or maid service, you report income and expenses on Schedule C, and self-employment tax may apply. Cleaning between stays is common in short-term rentals, so ask your preparer which applies.

Average stay of 7 days or less. Publication 925 explains that an activity where the average period of customer use is 7 days or less isn't treated as a "rental activity" under the passive activity rules. That can change how losses are treated. Your bookkeeping should let you compute average stay: total rented nights ÷ number of stays.

Form 1099-K. Platforms must send a Form 1099-K when payments exceed $20,000 in more than 200 transactions, and may send one for lower amounts. All income is reportable either way (IRS). The 1099-K usually shows gross amounts, which is another reason to keep your own gross-level records.

A monthly host bookkeeping routine

  1. Mark last month's completed stays and enter actual payouts.
  2. Resolve any payout-check differences.
  3. Log expenses from bank and card statements, tagged by listing.
  4. Record owner stays and blocked nights.
  5. Review occupancy, ADR and RevPAR by listing. Adjust pricing or minimum stays for the months ahead.
  6. Check the year-to-date P&L for each listing.

At year end, you'll have per-listing totals by category, rental days vs. personal-use days, and average stay length.

FAQ

How do you calculate Airbnb occupancy?

Booked guest nights divided by available nights, where available nights exclude owner stays and blocked nights.

What is ADR and RevPAR?

ADR (average daily rate) is accommodation revenue divided by booked nights, excluding cleaning fees. RevPAR is accommodation revenue divided by available nights.

Does an Airbnb go on Schedule E or Schedule C?

It depends on your facts. IRS Pub. 527 says providing substantial services such as regular cleaning or linen changes during a stay generally means Schedule C. Ask your tax professional.
