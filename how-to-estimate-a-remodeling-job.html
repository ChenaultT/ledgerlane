How to Estimate a Remodeling Job: A Step-by-Step Pricing Method for Small Contractors

Most underpriced remodeling jobs don't go wrong on the big items. They go wrong on the things nobody wrote down: the second trip to the supply house, the half day of demo that turned into a full day, the overhead that never made it into the number, or a "15% markup" that turned out to be a much thinner profit than expected.

This guide walks through a repeatable way to estimate a remodel, from walkthrough to signed quote, so every bid covers your real costs and leaves the profit you meant to earn. It works whether you estimate on paper, in a spreadsheet or in software.

Step 1: Pin down the scope before you price anything

You can't price what you haven't defined. On the walkthrough, write the scope room by room and trade by trade:

  • What's coming out (demo, haul-off, protection of floors and adjacent rooms)
  • What's going in (framing, plumbing, electrical, drywall, tile, cabinets, paint, fixtures, trim)
  • Who supplies what (owner-supplied fixtures, allowances for tile or lighting the client hasn't picked yet)
  • What's excluded (permits, structural surprises behind walls, hazardous material testing, appliance hookups)

Take photos and measurements. A written scope and exclusions list also protects you when a change order comes up.

Step 2: Break the job into line items and quantities

Turn the scope into line items, each with a unit and a quantity: square feet of tile, linear feet of trim, each for fixtures, hours for labor-only tasks. Group them by category (demo, framing, plumbing, electrical, drywall, flooring and so on). Grouping matters for two reasons: it makes missed items easier to spot, and it lets you compare budget against actual cost by category once the job is running.

Add a waste factor to materials where it applies (tile, flooring, drywall, trim). The right percentage depends on the material, the layout and your own history, so use what your past jobs tell you.

Step 3: Price materials with real numbers

Use current supplier pricing, not last year's memory. For each material line, include:

  • Unit cost from your supplier quote or recent receipts
  • Sales tax you'll pay on materials, if your state charges it to you (this is a cost to you, separate from any tax you charge the client; your state's rules decide which applies)
  • Delivery charges and small consumables (fasteners, adhesives, tape, blades) that are easy to forget

Step 4: Price labor at its burdened cost

A crew member's hourly wage is not what an hour of their time costs you. Your true hourly labor cost, often called the burdened rate, adds:

  • Employer payroll taxes. The employer share of Social Security and Medicare is 6.2% + 1.45% = 7.65% of wages (IRS Topic 751), plus federal and state unemployment taxes
  • Workers' compensation insurance (this varies a lot by state and job classification)
  • Benefits, paid time off and any vehicle or tool allowances

Example (illustrative numbers only): if wages are $30/hour and everything above adds up to 25% of wages, the burdened cost is $30 × 1.25 = $37.50/hour. Price labor at $37.50, not $30. Use your own payroll and insurance figures here.

Then estimate hours honestly. Include setup, cleanup, material runs and inspections, not just "tool time."

Step 5: Add subcontractors and equipment

Get written quotes from subs (electrician, plumber, drywall finisher) and enter them as their own lines. Add rental equipment (dumpster, floor sander, scaffolding) and disposal fees. These are direct job costs just like materials.

Step 6: Add contingency for the unknowns

Remodeling means opening walls and floors you can't see into. A contingency line covers the reasonable unknowns that sit inside your scope: a few extra sheets of drywall, a fitting you didn't plan for. It is not a substitute for change orders. Structural rot or a client who changes the tile after it's installed should be priced as a change order.

The right contingency depends on the age of the house and how much is hidden. Set it on purpose for each job instead of padding individual lines.

Step 7: Add overhead

Overhead is the cost of running the business that doesn't belong to any one job: insurance, truck payments, fuel, phone, software, accounting, office rent and your own time spent estimating and selling. A common approach is to total your annual overhead, divide by the direct job costs you expect to run in a year, and use that percentage on every estimate. Recalculate it at least once a year.

Step 8: Price for profit using margin, not markup

This is where many estimates go wrong. Markup is a percentage of your cost. Margin is a percentage of the selling price. They are not the same number.

If you want a 15% profit margin, the formula is:

Price = Total cost ÷ (1 − desired margin)

Adding 15% to your cost gives you a margin of only about 13%.

A worked example

Here is a small bathroom refresh with illustrative figures (not market prices):

Item Amount
Materials (incl. tax you pay) $4,000
Burdened labor $4,500
Subcontractors $1,500
Direct cost $10,000
Contingency (5% of direct cost) $500
Overhead (10% of cost + contingency) $1,050
Total cost $11,550
Price at a 15% margin: $11,550 ÷ 0.85 $13,588
Profit $2,038 (15.0% of price)

Compare that with adding a 15% markup to cost: $11,550 × 1.15 = $13,283. The profit is $1,733, which is only 13.0% of the price. That's roughly $300 less on one small job, and the gap grows with job size. A 15% margin is the same as a 17.6% markup on cost.

Step 9: Write a quote the client can understand

Clients don't need to see your cost lines. They need a one-page proposal with:

  • Scope by area or trade, in plain language
  • Allowances (e.g. tile at $X per square foot) and what happens if they choose something more expensive
  • Exclusions and assumptions
  • Price, any sales tax you charge them, and a payment schedule tied to milestones
  • How change orders work, and signature lines

Step 10: Track actual costs against the estimate

The estimate is a prediction. Job costing tells you whether it was right. As the job runs, log every receipt, sub invoice and payroll hour against the job and the same categories you estimated in. Compare budget vs. actual by category as you go, not just at the end. That's how you spot an overrun while there's still time to talk to the client, and how your next estimate gets more accurate.

Price approved change orders the same way you priced the original job (cost, contingency, overhead and margin) and add them to both the contract and the budget.
